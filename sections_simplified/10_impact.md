# 10. Environmental and Economic Impact: Why Physics Is Winning the Market 💰🌍

## The Argument Has Already Been Won — By Maths

The debate about renewable energy used to be framed as a choice between the economy and the environment. Either you care about the planet and accept higher energy costs, or you care about the economy and keep burning fossil fuels.

That trade-off no longer exists. In most countries today, solar and wind are simply **the cheapest way to make new electricity** — full stop. The environment wins as a side effect of the economics.

Here's how physics delivered this result.

---

## 10.1 The Energy Payback Myth: Busted 🔨

One of the most persistent arguments against solar panels is: "It takes more energy to make them than they ever produce."

This is false, and the data is clear.

**Lifecycle Analysis (LCA)** examines the total energy consumed across the entire life of a solar panel:
- Mining and refining silicon
- Manufacturing the cell, panel, and frame
- Transportation and installation
- Operating for 30 years
- Recycling at end of life

**The Result:**

| Panel Technology | Energy Payback Time |
|---|---|
| Standard silicon (monocrystalline) | 1.0–1.5 years |
| Polycrystalline silicon | 1.5–2.0 years |
| Thin-film (CdTe) | 0.5–1.0 year |
| Perovskite (projected) | < 0.3 years |

A modern panel pays back its manufacturing energy in **under 2 years** and then keeps operating for **25–30 years**. That's 27+ years of completely free, clean energy — a physical return on investment no fossil fuel can match, because coal and oil must be burned continuously to keep producing power.

---

## 10.2 LCOE: The Only Number That Matters 📉

**LCOE** stands for **Levelised Cost of Energy** — the total cost of building and running a power plant, divided by all the electricity it will ever produce, giving a cost per kilowatt-hour (kWh).

It accounts for everything: construction, fuel, maintenance, financing, and decommissioning — over the entire operating life of the plant.

### The Dramatic Shift in Solar LCOE

| Year | Solar PV Cost (LCOE, global average) |
|---|---|
| 2010 | ~$0.378 per kWh |
| 2015 | ~$0.128 per kWh |
| 2020 | ~$0.057 per kWh |
| 2024 | ~$0.040 per kWh |

**A 90% cost reduction in 14 years.** No other energy technology in history has ever done this.

Compare to fossil fuels:
- Natural gas (new plant): $0.065–0.095/kWh
- Coal (new plant): $0.065–0.155/kWh

In most of the world, it's now **cheaper to build a brand-new solar farm than to keep running an already-paid-off coal plant**. The physics of free fuel — sunlight costs $0 per tonne — is an economic force that can't be competed with.

### Why Does Solar Keep Getting Cheaper? Wright's Law

Solar follows **Wright's Law** (also called the learning rate): for every doubling of cumulative installed capacity, costs fall by a consistent percentage. For solar PV, this rate has been approximately **20%** per doubling since the 1980s.

This means the more solar we build, the cheaper it gets — and the cheaper it gets, the more we build. It's a virtuous cycle that accelerates itself, and we are clearly still on that curve.

## 10.3 Carbon Payback: What About Emissions? 🌿

Solar panels produce zero CO₂ during operation. But manufacturing them does produce some CO₂. How quickly does a solar panel "pay back" its carbon?

**Carbon Payback Period:**
- Standard silicon panel: **1 to 3 years** (depending on where electricity for manufacturing comes from)
- With renewable-powered manufacturing: under **6 months**

After that, every kWh of solar electricity produced displaces fossil fuel CO₂. A typical residential solar system in Bosnia will prevent the emission of approximately **8–12 tonnes of CO₂** over its 25-year lifetime — equivalent to taking one car off the road for 4–5 years.

## 10.4 Agrivoltaics: Double the Land Value 🌾☀️

A common criticism of large solar farms is land use. But a fascinating solution has emerged from agricultural physics: **Agrivoltaics** — growing crops underneath elevated solar panels.

**The Physics of Why It Works:**

Most crops can only use a fraction of full summer sunlight — they "saturate" at around 400–600 W/m² even though noon sunlight delivers 1000 W/m². The excess light just overheats the leaves and evaporates water (evapotranspiration).

Elevated panels provide **partial shade** that:
- Reduces water evaporation from soil and leaves by 20–30%
- Lowers plant heat stress on peak summer days
- Creates a cooler microclimate under the panels

The plants benefit from reduced stress and need less irrigation. In return, their water vapour release (**transpiration**) cools the solar panels above — and cooler panels (as we learned in Section 7) are more efficient.

**Documented results from agrivoltaic farms:**
- Solar electricity output: comparable to or slightly better than standard farms
- Crop yield: maintained or improved for shade-tolerant crops (leafy greens, berries, herbs)
- Water use: reduced by 20–30%
- Land efficiency: 60–70% more productive per hectare than either land use alone

This is not a future concept — agrivoltaic installations exist in Germany, Japan, South Korea, France, and are beginning to appear in the Balkans.

## 10.5 Jobs and Economic Development 💼

Beyond just electricity costs, the renewable energy sector creates local, non-exportable jobs:

| Sector | Type of Jobs |
|---|---|
| Solar manufacturing | Factory workers, engineers, material scientists |
| Installation | Electricians, roofers, project managers |
| Operation and maintenance | Technicians, data analysts |
| Research and development | Physicists, chemists, software engineers |

Unlike jobs in fossil fuel extraction (which require specific geographies and can be automated), solar and wind jobs are distributed — every community with sunshine or wind is a potential energy producer. For a country like Bosnia, rich in both solar radiation and technical education, this represents a genuine economic opportunity alongside environmental benefits.

## The Bottom Line

| Metric | Fossil Fuels | Solar + Wind |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel cost | Always increasing | Zero forever |
| Energy payback | N/A (continuously required) | 1–2 years, then free |
| Price trend | Volatile, often rising | Consistently falling |
| CO₂ emissions | High | Near-zero |
| Land use | Often remote extraction | Local, dual-use possible |
| Local jobs | Often centralised | Distributed, local |

The physics of free fuel combined with exponentially improving technology has made renewables not just the right choice for the climate — but the obvious rational choice for any economy planning its energy future.
