# 2. Overview of Renewable Energy Sources

Renewable energy systems harness energy from continuous, naturally replenishing processes. While their manifestations differ, the extraction of useful work from these sources relies on fundamental physical principles, primarily thermodynamics, fluid mechanics, and electromagnetism.

### 2.1 Mechanical Energy Harvesting: Wind and Hydro

Wind and hydroelectric power rely on the conversion of kinetic and potential energy into electrical energy using turbines and generators. 

**Wind Energy** is governed by fluid dynamics. The kinetic energy $E_k$ of a moving air mass is proportional to the cube of the wind velocity $v$. The power $P$ available in the wind is given by:
$$ P = \frac{1}{2} \rho A v^3 $$
where $\rho$ is the air density and $A$ is the swept area of the turbine blades. However, according to **Betz's Law**, no wind turbine can capture more than 16/27 (approximately 59.3%) of the kinetic energy in the wind, a fundamental limit derived from the conservation of mass and momentum.

**Hydroelectric Power** relies on the gravitational potential energy of water. The power generated is a function of the hydraulic head (height difference) and the volumetric flow rate, governed by Bernoulli's principle. 

### 2.2 Thermodynamic Systems: Geothermal and Biomass

**Geothermal Energy** utilizes the thermal gradient between the Earth's core and its surface. The extraction of this heat follows the laws of thermodynamics. High-enthalpy resources use steam directly to drive turbines, while low-enthalpy resources employ binary cycle power plants using organic Rankine cycles. The maximum theoretical efficiency of these systems is dictated by Carnot's theorem, limiting the conversion of thermal energy to mechanical work based on the temperature difference between the heat source and the heat sink.

**Biomass** involves the release of stored chemical energy through combustion or biochemical conversion. This energy originally originates from solar radiation captured via photosynthesis. The efficiency of biomass power plants is similarly constrained by thermodynamic cycles (e.g., the Rankine cycle for steam turbines).

### 2.3 Direct Conversion: Solar Energy

Unlike mechanical or thermodynamic systems, solar photovoltaics (PV) convert electromagnetic radiation directly into electrical current without moving parts or intermediate thermal cycles. This process, governed by quantum mechanics and solid-state physics, avoids the Carnot cycle limits inherent in thermal power plants. However, it is subject to its own fundamental physical constraints, such as the Shockley-Queisser limit, which we will explore in subsequent sections. The direct nature of this conversion makes solar PV one of the most scalable and elegant renewable technologies.
